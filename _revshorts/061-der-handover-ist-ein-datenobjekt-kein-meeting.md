Kickoff call, week one after signing. The new CSM introduces himself and asks the customer to briefly explain, once more, what he actually wants to achieve. The customer pauses. He explained all of this to sales, in detail, across three discovery calls, there are recordings, there are notes. Now he tells it a second time, a bit shorter, a bit more annoyed, quietly wondering whether this shop even talks to itself internally.

The usual answer to this problem is called the handover meeting. Thirty minutes, the AE talks, the CSM types along, half of it gets lost, because that is how memory works. Three weeks later the AE is deep in the next deal and practically unreachable for follow-up questions. The meeting was well intentioned. It just does not scale, and it leaves nothing behind that anyone can look up.

The better approach: the handover is a data object. Four blocks, as required fields on the opportunity or as its own object in the CRM. Goals, meaning what the customer wants to have achieved in twelve months, in his own words. Promises, meaning what sales committed to, including the verbal commitments from the second-to-last call that never make it into the contract and cause the trouble later. Stakeholders, meaning who signed, who works with it daily, and who internally never wanted the project in the first place. And risks, from the tight budget to the champion who is currently changing jobs.

The trick is in the timing. Closed Won only goes through when these fields are filled, exactly as mandatory as amount and close date. The AE documents while the deal is fresh and the details are still in his head, not three weeks later from faded memory. From there the object travels automatically: into the onboarding project, into the CS view, into the first QBR template. If you run Gong or a comparable tool, you have the raw version pre-filled from the call transcripts and only edit.

You can still hold the meeting. It is just no longer a data transfer, it is a conversation about what is already documented. Sounds like a small difference. For the customer it is huge: he learns in the first week whether he is dealing with one company or with two departments sharing a mailbox.
