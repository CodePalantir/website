Ask your best salesperson about their top deals and pay attention not to what they say, but to what they open. If it's a spreadsheet named "Pipeline_Mine_v3.xlsx," you've just met your company's real CRM.

The shadow spreadsheets are everywhere. The sales lead maintains his forecast in Google Sheets because the CRM forecasting "doesn't reflect how we think." CS tracks onboardings in a spreadsheet with 14 tabs and conditional formatting that three people work in at the same time. Marketing keeps a campaign list that gets reconciled with the CRM by hand roughly once a quarter. Every one of these spreadsheets exists because the system can't do something or makes it too cumbersome.

That's exactly where it gets interesting. The usual reflex is a ban: everything belongs in the CRM, spreadsheets are prohibited effective immediately. Never works. The spreadsheets go underground, get labeled "private," and get reconciled even less than before. You fight the symptom and lose the information.

The better move: read the spreadsheets like feature requests. Because that's what they are. Every column that exists in the Excel file and not in the CRM is a missing field. Every sort order someone built for themselves is a missing list view. Every VLOOKUP construction across two tabs is an integration someone runs by hand, every week, for months. People are documenting for you, precisely, what the system lacks. They're just not writing it in the ticket system, they're writing it in cell B2.

A shadow spreadsheet is the most honest requirements catalog you'll ever get. More honest than any workshop, because nobody plays meeting-room politics over a private spreadsheet. It shows what someone really needs to do their job, backed by the effort they invest voluntarily.

So, as a process: actively hunt for shadow spreadsheets, explicitly without blame. Three questions per find. What data is in here that's missing from the CRM? What view does the spreadsheet offer that the system doesn't? What would have to happen for you to delete it voluntarily? The answers are your ops backlog for the next two quarters, pre-prioritized. Because what someone builds for themselves in Excel is something they genuinely need.

The spreadsheets disappear on their own, by the way, as soon as the system is better than the workaround. Not before. No matter what you ban.

How many shadow spreadsheets are running in your company right now? If you don't know: that's an answer too.
